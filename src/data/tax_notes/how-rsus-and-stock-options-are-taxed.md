# How RSUs and stock options are taxed

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc427 | Finnie note, 2026-11-25 -->

Equity pay from an employer is taxed in two stages: first as **wages** when you actually receive something of value, then as a **capital gain or loss** when you sell the shares. The rules differ by type.

## Restricted stock units (RSUs)

An RSU is a promise to give you shares once you meet a vesting condition (usually staying with the company for a time).

- **At grant:** no tax.
- **At vesting:** the market value of the shares you receive is **ordinary wage income**, shown on your W-2, with income tax, Social Security, and Medicare due. Employers often withhold by keeping some of the shares ("sell to cover"), commonly at the 22% supplemental rate, which can be less than your actual bracket.
- **At sale:** your cost basis is the value at vesting. Any further rise is a capital gain, **short-term** if you sell within a year of vesting and **long-term** after that. Selling right at vesting usually means little or no extra tax.

A common mistake is reporting the broker's basis of $0 on Form 1099-B, which taxes the same income twice; the correct basis is the value already taxed on your W-2.

## Non-qualified stock options (NSOs)

- **At exercise:** the "spread" (market value minus your exercise price) is ordinary wage income.
- **At sale:** gain or loss after exercise is a capital gain or loss, based on the market value at exercise.

## Incentive stock options (ISOs)

- **At exercise:** no regular income tax, but the spread counts for the **alternative minimum tax** and can trigger it.
- **Qualifying sale:** if you hold the shares at least **two years from the grant** and **one year from exercise**, the whole gain over the exercise price is a long-term capital gain.
- **Disqualifying sale** (sooner): the spread at exercise becomes ordinary income, the rest a capital gain.

## Employee stock purchase plans (ESPPs)

Qualified plans let you buy shares at up to a **15% discount**. There's no tax when you buy; when you sell, part of the gain (based on the discount) is ordinary income and the rest a capital gain, with better treatment if you hold the shares two years from the offering date and one year from purchase.

## Example

100 RSUs vest when the stock is $50: $5,000 is added to your wages. You sell them 18 months later at $70: a $2,000 long-term capital gain ($7,000 − $5,000 basis).
