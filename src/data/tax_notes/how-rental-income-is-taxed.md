# How rental income is taxed

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527 | Finnie note, 2026-11-25 -->

If you rent out a house, apartment, or room, the rent you receive is taxable income, but you can deduct the costs of renting it out, including a yearly allowance for the building's wear and tear (**depreciation**). Most individual landlords report this on **Schedule E**.

## Income

Rental income includes regular rent, advance rent (taxed when received), fees a tenant pays you, and the value of services a tenant provides instead of rent. A refundable security deposit is not income unless you keep it.

## Deductible expenses

For the rental part of a property you can deduct, among others: mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, repairs and maintenance, utilities you pay, property management fees, advertising, travel to the property, and legal and accounting fees. **Improvements** (a new roof, an addition) are not deducted at once; they are depreciated over time.

## Depreciation

You recover the cost of a residential rental **building** (not the land) by deducting it over **27.5 years**: a $275,000 building gives about $10,000 a year. Depreciation lowers your taxable rental profit each year, but it is "recaptured" when you sell: the gain equal to the depreciation you took is taxed at up to 25%.

## Losses and the passive activity rules

Rental activity is generally **passive**, so a rental loss can't normally offset wages or other income. The main exception: if you **actively participate** (you make decisions such as approving tenants and setting rent), you can deduct up to **$25,000** of rental losses against other income. That allowance shrinks by $1 for every $2 of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000 and is gone at $150,000. Losses you can't use carry forward to future years and to the year you sell. Real estate professionals have different rules.

## Short rentals and personal use

- Renting a home you also live in for **fewer than 15 days** a year: the rent is not taxable and the rental expenses are not deductible.
- A vacation home used both personally and as a rental must split its expenses between the two uses, and deductions for the rental part may be limited to the rental income.

## Example

A landlord collects $24,000 of rent and pays $6,000 of mortgage interest, $3,000 of property tax, $1,500 of insurance, and $2,500 of repairs, and takes $9,000 of depreciation. Taxable rental profit is $24,000 − $22,000 = $2,000, added to her other income.
