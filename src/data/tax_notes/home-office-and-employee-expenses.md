# Home office and work expenses for employees

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc509 | Finnie note, 2026-11-26 -->

Whether you can deduct a home office, furniture, a computer or other work costs depends on how you are paid. **Employees paid on a W-2 generally can't deduct them**; **self-employed people can**.

## W-2 employees

Costs an employee pays for their job and the employer doesn't repay are called **unreimbursed employee expenses**: a home office, office furniture and equipment, a phone or internet used for work, tools, uniforms, work travel. Before 2018 they could be itemized (only the part above 2% of adjusted gross income). The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act **suspended** this deduction for 2018 through 2025, and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 **ended it permanently**. Working from home, even full time and at the employer's request, doesn't change this.

What an employee can do instead:

- Ask the employer to **reimburse** the costs under an accountable plan: a reimbursement is not taxable income.
- A few groups still deduct some work costs: Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state or local officials, and employees with impairment-related work expenses. Teachers (K-12 educators) can deduct up to **$300** of classroom costs above the line.

## Self-employed people

If you run a business (freelance, contractor, sole owner), you deduct ordinary and necessary business costs on **Schedule C**, including a **home office** used regularly and only for the business. Two ways to work it out:

- **Simplified method**: $5 per square foot of office, up to 300 square feet (at most $1,500 a year).
- **Regular method**: the office's share of the home's costs (rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs, depreciation), by its share of the floor area.

Furniture and equipment for the business can be deducted too, often all in the year bought.

## Example

John works remotely as a W-2 employee and spends $3,000 on a desk, chair and monitor. He can't deduct any of it on his federal return. If he were a freelancer using a dedicated home office, the $3,000 would be a business expense on Schedule C, lowering both income tax and self-employment tax.
