# The wash sale rule

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550 | Finnie note, 2026-11-26 -->

Selling an investment at a loss usually lets you subtract the loss from your gains, and up to $3,000 a year from other income. The **wash sale rule** stops this when you sell at a loss and buy the same thing back almost at once: the IRS treats it as if you never really sold.

## When it applies

A sale is a wash sale if, **within 30 days before or after** selling at a loss (a 61-day window), you, your spouse, or an account you control (an IRA included):

- buy the same stock, bond, fund or option, or one that is **substantially identical** to it, or
- get a contract or option to buy it.

It only applies to **losses**. Selling at a gain and buying back is fine. Two different funds that track different indexes are generally not substantially identical; two share classes of the same fund, or the same company's common stock, are.

## What happens to the loss

The loss is **not deductible now, but it is not lost**. It is added to the cost basis of the shares you bought back, and their holding period includes the time you held the old shares. When you later sell the new shares, the higher basis makes the gain smaller or the loss bigger.

If the shares are bought back inside an **IRA**, the loss is lost for good: an IRA's basis doesn't carry it.

## Example

You buy 100 shares for $7,000 and sell them for $3,000, a $4,000 loss. A week later you buy 100 shares of the same stock for $3,200. The $4,000 loss is disallowed; the new shares' basis becomes $3,200 + $4,000 = $7,200. If you later sell them for $5,000, your loss then is $2,200, and it can be deducted if you don't buy back again within 30 days.

## Avoiding it

Wait at least 31 days before buying the same investment back, or buy a similar but not substantially identical one (for example a fund tracking a different index) to stay invested. Brokers report wash sales they see in the same account on Form 1099-B, but not across your accounts at other firms or in an IRA.
