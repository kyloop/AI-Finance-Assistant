# How cryptocurrency is taxed

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets | Finnie note, 2026-11-25 -->

For US federal tax, cryptocurrency and other digital assets (including stablecoins and NFTs) are treated as **property**, not as currency. That means the same rules as stocks and other investments: you owe tax when you have a gain, and you can deduct losses.

## When you owe tax

A taxable event happens when you:

- **sell** crypto for dollars,
- **exchange** one crypto for another (for example, bitcoin for ether),
- **spend** crypto on goods or services, which counts as selling it at its market value,
- **receive** crypto as income: pay for work, mining and staking rewards, and most airdrops are ordinary income at their market value when you receive them.

Simply buying crypto with dollars, holding it, or moving it between your own wallets is not taxable.

## Gains and losses

Your gain or loss is the value you received minus your **cost basis** (what you paid, plus fees). As with stocks:

- held **one year or less**: a **short-term** gain, taxed at your regular income-tax rate;
- held **more than one year**: a **long-term** gain, taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax at higher incomes.

Losses offset gains, and up to **$3,000** a year of net loss can offset other income, with the rest carried forward. Keeping records of each purchase (date, amount, price) matters, because each sale needs its own basis.

## Reporting

- Every Form 1040 asks whether you received, sold, exchanged, or otherwise disposed of a digital asset during the year; you must answer yes or no.
- Sales go on **Form 8949** and **Schedule D**; crypto income goes on Schedule 1 (or Schedule C if it's a business).
- Brokers and exchanges now send **Form 1099-DA**: it reports gross proceeds for sales from 2025, and cost basis as well for assets bought from 2026 on through the same broker.

## Example

You bought 1 ether for $2,000 in March 2025 and used it to buy a laptop in June 2026 when it was worth $3,500. Spending it is a sale: a $1,500 long-term gain (held more than a year), taxed at 15% for most people, or $225.
