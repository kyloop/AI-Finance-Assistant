# Selling your home and taxes

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523 | Finnie note, 2026-11-25 -->

Profit from selling your main home is a capital gain, but most homeowners pay no tax on it because of the **home sale exclusion** (Internal Revenue Code section 121).

## The exclusion

You can leave out of your income up to **$250,000** of gain from selling your main home, or **$500,000** if you are married filing jointly. These amounts are set in the law and are not adjusted for inflation. The gain is the selling price, minus selling costs (agent commissions, closing costs), minus your cost basis: what you paid for the home plus the cost of improvements such as a new roof, an addition, or a remodeled kitchen. Ordinary repairs and maintenance do not add to the basis.

## The two-out-of-five-years test

To use the full exclusion you must have **owned** the home and **lived in it as your main home for at least two years** during the five years before the sale. The two years don't have to be in a row. For the $500,000 amount, either spouse can meet the ownership test but both must meet the use test. You can use the exclusion only **once every two years**.

If you don't meet the test because you had to move for a new job, health reasons, or certain unforeseen events, you may still get a **partial exclusion**, in proportion to the time you did meet it.

## When part of the gain is taxable

- Gain above $250,000 / $500,000 is taxed as a capital gain: long-term rates (0%, 15%, or 20%) if you owned the home more than a year, and possibly the 3.8% net investment income tax at higher incomes.
- If you rented the home out or used part of it as a home office and claimed **depreciation**, that depreciation is taxed when you sell (at up to 25%), even if the rest of the gain is excluded.
- Periods when the home was not your main residence (for example, a rental before you moved in) can make part of the gain taxable.

## Losses

A loss on selling your personal home **cannot be deducted**. Only a loss on investment or rental property can.

## Reporting

If all of your gain is excluded and you didn't receive a Form 1099-S, you usually don't have to report the sale. Otherwise it goes on Form 8949 and Schedule D.

## Example

A married couple bought their house for $300,000, spent $50,000 on a new kitchen, and sell it for $900,000 after living there eight years, paying $50,000 in selling costs. Their gain is $900,000 − $50,000 − $350,000 = $500,000. Filing jointly, all $500,000 is excluded, so they owe no federal income tax on the sale.
