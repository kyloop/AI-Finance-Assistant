# How dividends and interest are taxed

<!-- https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc404 | Finnie note, 2026-11-25 -->

Investment income that you receive while holding an investment, rather than profit from selling it, is taxed in a few different ways depending on what it is. Your bank or broker reports it to you and the IRS on Form 1099-INT (interest) and Form 1099-DIV (dividends).

## Interest

Interest from savings accounts, money market accounts, certificates of deposit (CDs), bonds, and loans you make is **ordinary income**: it is taxed at your regular income-tax bracket (10% to 37%), the same as wages. Interest is taxable in the year it is credited to your account, even if you leave it there.

- **US Treasury interest** (Treasury bills, notes, bonds, I bonds, and savings bonds) is taxed by the federal government but is **exempt from state and local income tax**.
- **Municipal bond interest** from bonds issued by states and cities is generally **exempt from federal income tax**, and often from your own state's tax if the bond is from your state. It still counts when working out how much of your Social Security benefits is taxable, and interest on some "private activity" bonds counts for the alternative minimum tax.
- Interest earned inside a 401(k), IRA, or HSA is not taxed each year; the account's own rules apply when you withdraw.

## Dividends

Dividends are payments a company or fund makes to its shareholders. There are two kinds:

- **Qualified dividends** are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates: **0%, 15%, or 20%**, depending on your taxable income. Most dividends from US companies and many foreign companies qualify if you held the shares for **more than 60 days** during the 121-day period that starts 60 days before the ex-dividend date.
- **Ordinary (non-qualified) dividends** are taxed at your regular income-tax bracket. These include dividends on shares you held too briefly, most dividends from real estate investment trusts (REITs), and distributions from money market funds.

Mutual funds and ETFs also pass along **capital gain distributions** when they sell holdings at a profit; these are taxed as long-term capital gains whatever your holding period in the fund. Your Form 1099-DIV shows each type separately.

## The extra 3.8% at higher incomes

Above $200,000 of modified adjusted gross income ($250,000 married filing jointly), interest, dividends, and capital gains can also owe the **3.8% net investment income tax**.

## Example

A single person with $70,000 of wages receives $1,000 of savings interest and $2,000 of qualified dividends. The interest is taxed at her 22% bracket ($220). The qualified dividends fall in the 15% capital gains band ($300). Had the dividends been ordinary, they would have been taxed at 22% ($440).
